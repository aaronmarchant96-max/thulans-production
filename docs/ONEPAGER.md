# THE THULANS
*An industrial tragedy about who gets to decide what another person must endure.*

---

**The logline:**
Three hundred years after he was sealed inside a walking iron rescue machine, a man wakes
into the mountain city he once served and finds it dying. Its rulers have learned to fake
miracles — hiding the water and ore that remains, then accepting a volunteer's life and
revealing the find as an answer to blood. When he proves the miracles were staged, the
truth does not free anyone. It destroys the only thing holding the city together: the lie.

---

**The tragic engine:**
Varek was right about the lie and wrong about the cost of exposing it. The lie was
monstrous. The lie was also load-bearing. He gave people freedom without giving them a way
to survive it.

**Thematic backbone:** A civilization can survive the exhaustion of a mountain. What it
cannot survive indefinitely is turning every form of love and sacrifice into a pricing
mechanism.

**The setting's central distinction: the scarcity is real. The causal story is
fraudulent.**

---

## Varek
Eight feet of permanently sealed cast iron, hydraulics, and riveted boiler-plate — a
structural rescue anchor built three hundred years ago. Inside is a mortal man: aged,
scarred, and slowly going blind, kept alive by the machine he wears.

He wakes believing something of his home survived. What he finds is that his people
survived biologically while the moral world he remembered died.

> His language survived. Its moral grammar was inverted.

His story is personal before it is political: he stops trying to recover a lost
civilization and begins protecting the actual people standing in front of him.

> "I had a mother."

## The Mountain
*Fairguni* — a continent-sized mountain city, three thousand years old, where an entire
civilization lives inside the stone and measures space up and down. From the clean
administrative tiers to the working drifts of the lower Hearths, across a 2,000-meter
industrial chasm.

## The Vertical Economy
Hearth Marks. Provision entitlements. Marrow contracts. The Life Tithe. A civilization
that learned to price everything a person can give — up to and including the self — while
calling the last step sacred.

> "A body may be given. It should not have to be sold."

## Wulfila's Tithe
A sincere volunteer whose district climbed the whole ladder — overtime, tools, marks,
entitlements, debt, marrow — before the state asked for the last thing. He took the logic
seriously and offered his life. The water existed. The aquifer had been surveyed and its
release deliberately delayed.

## The Reveal
When Wulfila's death is exposed as manufactured, the truth travels through surveyors,
shift boards, manifests, guild presses, and ordinary logistics. Over weeks, not days, it
destroys the legitimacy of state and religion — and slow decline becomes immediate
collapse.

---

## The ending

> "Did the man on the statue die for the Spire?"
> "No. It was our iron anyway."
> — Sanna, six years after the dismantling

No restoration. No clean revolution. The macro ending is catastrophic; the micro ending
contains life. Truthful obligation returns to the Hearth. Varek gives his machine's parts
to the people who built it, and dies as an ordinary man holding Sanna's hand.

Sealed in an iron rescue machine for three centuries, a man wakes to a dying city whose
rulers fake miracles to make the faithful volunteer to die — and the truth he carries may
destroy the only thing holding it together.
