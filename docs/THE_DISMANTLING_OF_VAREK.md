# The Dismantling of Varek Fairgunjis

---

### 1. The Office on Tier Nine

The door was pine planks set into old mortar. When Varek leaned his shoulder against the latch, the screws pulled out with a dry scrape and the wood swung in, banging once against the stone wall.

Theodemir didn’t jump. He was seventy years old, sitting on a three-legged bench with a slate across his knees and an iron stylus between two fingers stained dark with lampblack. His wool tunic had two grease patches on the chest and the sleeves were worn down to the warp at the elbows.

"You're trailing oil," Theodemir said without looking up from the numbers. "Gero’s boy scrubbed that pine yesterday."

Varek stood in the doorframe. The three-tonne caisson filled the opening from jamb to lintel. The Fault Maul hung from his left fist, the counter-pick spotted with orange rust where the hydraulic seal had wept over the joint. His right arm was dead at his side, tied to his belt hoop with two fathoms of tarred hemp, the copper coils inside the housing fused into a lump of black glass that gave off the sour smell of burned varnish.

Inside his breastplate, the auxiliary air pump gave a wet, three-stroke rattle.

"Shaft 44," Varek said. The bronze throat-plate set into his neck made the words come out flat and heavy, vibrating through the pine floorboards. "You had the acoustic trace on the nineteenth."

Theodemir laid the stylus into the groove cut along the slate’s edge. He didn't reach for a bell and he didn't stand up. He reached behind his bench to the second shelf of tablets, took down two pieces of dressed slate, and laid them side by side on the table.

The first slate had a red wax seal pressed into the corner:

`SHAFT 44 // ACOUSTIC SHEAR PREDICTION: D-19`  
`COPPER DRAW REQUIRED: 820 TONS`  
`PROJECTED CASUALTY BUDGET: 38–45 HEWERS`

The second slate was older, gray stone smoothed down with river sand:

`AZG-HAÍMS // AQUIFER SURVEY // CITIZEN WULFILA`

"Forty days before Wulfila took the leap off the gallows platform," Theodemir said. His voice was dry, the sound of two pieces of slate rubbing together in a pocket. "The north cistern had eleven million gallons in reserve. We had the sounding on the third of the month."

Varek’s chest engine missed a stroke, hissed, caught again. 

He remembered the gallows platform on Tier Six. The Ministry of Faith had ordered him to stand behind the boy—three tonnes of ancestral iron to show the crowd that the First Hearth approved of the tithe. Varek had stood like a statue in the cold draft while Wulfila’s girl Haila tied the hammer-ring to the boy's cord. Wulfila had looked back once, right into Varek's quartz slit, believing his death would buy a water exemption for his family's hearth. 

And then the Council drafted the boy's fourteen-year-old brother Bram into the deep copper drifts anyway.

"He jumped for nothing," Varek rasped.

"He jumped so forty thousand men would keep cutting timber," Theodemir said. He picked up the stylus again, turned it between his thumb and forefinger, and set it down. "If Azg-Haíms knew there was water, they’d have struck for twelve-hour shifts instead of sixteen. The timber flumes would have run dry. When the flumes run dry, the blast furnaces on Tier Four go cold. When the furnaces go cold, the hull plates for Ship Three don't get rolled. You want me to tell forty thousand timbermen the mountain’s coming down in twenty years? Try it. Watch how many props get notched tomorrow morning."

The Archon got up, walked to the large slate hung from iron hooks on the back wall, and picked up a nub of chalk.

"The mantle is sixty miles thick under the Sinks. Three months ago the floor of Drift Twelve rose four inches. Not rock heave. Magma displacement. The chamber’s coming up at forty yards a year."

He drew a triangle on the slate, split it with a horizontal scratch, and rubbed the chalk dust off his palm onto his hip.

"Twenty-eight years if Kessel’s acoustic readings are right. Eleven if the lower basalt is as thin as the survey says. We have four point two million living souls in this mountain—rattling around inside infrastructure built for ten times that number. And we have six hulls on the slips. With double-stacked bunks and two pints of gray water a week, they hold six hundred thousand people. That’s every bureaucrat, every machinist with a master certificate, every woman under thirty with two working lungs, and enough foundry boys to keep the scrubbers turning. That leaves three point six million people in the drifts. One in seven gets a berth, Varek. Who do you want me to cross off first?"

Theodemir turned around. His eyes were small, watery, yellowed at the corners by seventy years of bad oil lamps.

"You came up the steps to hit me with that hammer," the old man said. "Go ahead. Crack the skull. Gero comes down from the fifth desk at four o'clock, sits on this bench, and signs the shift manifests with the same ink. The math doesn't give a damn about your hammer, Varek."

Varek looked at the table. He looked at the red wax on the Shaft 44 tablet. His left shoulder joint was whining, the gear teeth slipping three degrees every time he took a breath.

His fingers opened.

The thirty-kilo maul slipped from the steel grip. It hit the pine planks flat, biting half an inch into the dry wood with a heavy, dead thump that shook the inkwells on the shelves.

He turned his back on the old man and took two steps toward the dark stairwell.

"Where are you going?" Theodemir called.

The bronze plate in Varek's throat hummed against his collar:

"Down."

### 2. Lower Four

The sub-drift of Lower Hearth Four smelled of stale ditch water, wet wool, and carbolic soap.

In the dry corner, three infants lay inside split dynamite crates lined with old flour sacking. Their breathing was thin and whistling, the sound of wet leather rubbing on stone. Beside them, on two red coal clinkers set into an iron pan, a tin kettle was simmering wild leek greens and mushroom stems with a sprig of mountain pine. The tin rim of the pot had been wiped clean with a damp rag, bright metal showing through the soot.

Kaelen the drift smith was kneeling in three inches of greasy mud by the drainage ditch, an eighteen-inch monkey wrench in his hand. Between his boots lay the carcass of a four-inch cast-iron bilge pump. The impeller housing was cracked down the middle and the bronze vanes were ground flat by grit.

"The shaft’s bent, Sanna," Kaelen said, wiping his nose with the back of a greasy wrist. "The third-tier store wants fourteen hundredweight of scrap copper for a new sleeve. We don’t have fourteen ounces."

Sanna sat on an upturned nail keg by the crates. Her apron was gray with carbolic wash, her fingers red and split around the cuticles. Her brother Bram was lying behind her on a heap of wet shale sacks, his broken shin tied up between two pine laths with hemp string, teeth chattering every time a drop of cold water hit his neck.

"Water's up an inch since second bell," she said. "If it hits the straw, the babies won't last the night shift."

The floorboards in the entry drift groaned.

Varek came through the timber frame sideways, ducking his iron visor under the lintel. His dead right arm was still lashed to his waist.

He looked at the mud. He looked at the cracked cast-iron casing between Kaelen’s boots.

He reached down with his left gauntlet, got three fingers behind the high-pressure union of his secondary hydraulic return line, and pulled.

The brass fitting tore with a wet crack. Amber mineral oil sprayed across the wall timbers, smelling of hot kerosene and machine fat. Varek hauled the small rotary pump out of its mounting bracket, sheared the copper supply line across his knee brace, and dropped the bronze assembly into the ditch at Kaelen’s feet.

"Four-inch intake," Varek said. The bronze plate in his throat scraped like a shovel on gravel. "Bronze vanes. It'll throw forty gallons a minute."

Kaelen dropped his wrench into the mud. "Varek... that's your left stabilizer return. You won't bend the knee."

Varek turned around and sat down on a stack of rot-blackened larch ties in the dry corner. The timber gave a long, splintering groan under the three tonnes of cast iron. His left leg stiffened out straight as the last of the hydraulic oil drained into the ditch.

He leaned his helmet back against the rock rib.

"Bring the wrenches," he said.

### 3. The Unbolting

They stripped him the way timbermen strip an old steam winch before a ceiling falls.

By midnight there were thirty people in the drift. They had cold chisels, rusted flat spanners, two dull hacksaws, and three wooden grease firkins salvaged from the hoist house.

Kaelen worked the thirty-millimeter hex bolts on the left thigh casing. The first bolt took three men on a cheater pipe to budge; the second sheared off in the hole, the wrench slipping and peeling two inches of skin off Kaelen’s wrist. He didn't stop. He wiped his forearm on his trousers and called for the chisel.

"Get the tub under," Kaelen barked at Jarek. "Don’t spill the grease. That’s clean cylinder oil."

The boy shoved the wooden firkin under the casing. The warm, yellow oil came out in a thick rope. A woman with gray hair and bad teeth immediately dipped a torn piece of flannel into the tub, wringing it out into a tin lamp to keep the drift lit.

"The shin plates," Varek said from inside the iron. "Take the cold chisel to the flange rivets."

Jarek and an older hewer named Tomas set their chisels behind the heavy rolled plates. They hit them with eight-pound sledges until the iron popped free with a sound like pistol shots. Two hundred pounds of boiler plate hit the mud. Within three minutes, four timbermen were dragging the iron out down the drift to wedge over a broken cap-piece at Station Six.

Other pieces didn't go so clean.

Tomas unthreaded the bronze canister from the right collar—the closed-circuit air scrubber, packed with activated charcoal and three-hundred-year-old silver-mesh filters.

"For the bunkhouse flues!" Tomas yelled, shoving through the crowd with the canister under his arm.

Five minutes later he was back, swearing in the tunnel. The flues on Tier Six were square-rolled sheet iron; the scrubber had an imperial bayonet thread with three locking lugs. They didn't have a transition flange. While two men were trying to beat the round bronze collar into a square opening with a sledge, the canister slipped on the greasy stones. The bottom seam split. Three centuries of scrubbed mountain air—dry, cold, smelling of old snow and high rock—hissed out into the sulfur ditch in four seconds.

Nobody said anything. Tomas stood looking down at the dented bronze in the mud, kicked it against the rock wall, and walked out into the dark.

In the entryway, two women were rolling on the gravel, clawing at each other over an eight-inch copper cooling elbow. Kaelen had to wade in with his monkey wrench and knock one of them into the wall to get them apart.

Varek watched through the slit in his visor. He didn't move his head.

Kaelen crawled out from under the suit’s chest plate carrying the heavy wool lining—three layers of quilted gray felt lined with lead foil, still warm from the engine cycle. He dropped it into Sanna’s lap.

"For Bram," Kaelen said, his face black with oil. "His feet are blue. Wrap him."

Sanna looked at the wool. It smelled of mineral oil, lead, and old wool. She looked at Bram lying against the timber props with his broken leg propped on a sack, shivering so hard his teeth clicked.

Then she looked at the three dynamite boxes in the mud, where the six babies were coughing in their damp rags.

She stood up, walked past her brother’s boots, and spread the heavy warm felt across the three boxes, tucking the corners down around the wood.

Bram watched her from his straw. He didn't say anything. He pulled his thin gray blanket up to his throat and closed his eyes.

Kaelen climbed up on the larch ties behind Varek’s shoulder, his wrench resting against the heavy bronze collar that locked the life-support pipe into the back of the helmet.

"That's the last line, Varek," the smith said, his voice dropping low. "The auxiliary pumps are stripped. If I break this neck union, the lung-circuit vents. You'll be breathing the ditch."

Varek’s chest gave a dry, rattling sigh.

"Who cast the hip pins, Kaelen?"

"My grandfather," Kaelen muttered, staring at the hex nuts.

"The iron was never mine," Varek said. "Take them off."

### 4. The Man Inside

By four in the morning, the iron was gone.

What was left on the larch ties looked like something dragged out of a collapsed cellar.

He weighed seventy pounds. His legs were as thin as spruce poles, white and hairless, scarred with deep purple ruts where the steel clamps had held his thighs for three centuries. His linen under-suit had rotted into yellow strings across his ribs. Along his hips and spine, the skin was broken open into raw gray sores where the harness had rubbed through to the gristle.

The eighty-pound helmet was sitting upside down in the mud like an empty iron tub.

When the sulfur air of the drift hit his throat, Varek coughed—a thin, bubbling rattle that brought up a smear of black phlegm onto his sparse white beard. The smell that came off him was thick and foul: old urine, stale grease, three hundred years of dead skin trapped against rubber seals.

The miners who had been shouting over the scrap fell back against the timbers. Two of them took off their caps.

Varek was shivering. His cataracts made his eyes look like boiled onions in the flare of the grease lamps.

Down in the ditch, the bronze rotary pump was humming with a steady, smooth *thrum-thrum-thrum*. The water was already down four inches.

Sanna brought an old tin mug of warm kettle water, holding it with both hands. She sat on the nail keg beside the wood ties and lifted the rim to his mouth.

He took two swallows. Most of it ran down through his beard onto his chest, but his throat clicked twice.

"Bram?" he whispered. Without the bronze plate, his voice was just an old man's wheeze.

"Asleep," she said. "The leg's straight. He'll mend."

Varek looked down at his right hand lying on his knee. The knuckles were swollen with gout, the fingernails thick and yellow like horn. For three hundred years, those fingers had lived inside an electric glove. If he had touched a child's shoulder, the servo would have broken the collarbone.

Sanna reached out and took the old man's hand in hers.

Her fingers were rough, scarred by lye and carbolic, but they were hot with blood.

Varek’s hand twitched once in her grip. He didn't pull away. He looked down at the two hands together—the black grime under her nails, the liver spots across his skin.

"You're cold," she said.

Varek let his head rest back against the rock. His breath came out slow, smelling of old iron and tea.

"No," he said. "It's all right."

He closed his eyes. His chest lifted once, stayed still, and didn't fall.

### 5. The Concourse

They carried him down into the footwall workings before shift bell, wrapped in two dirty flour sacks. Four timbermen dug out an old tool pocket in the granite, laid him inside, and shoveled three feet of loose shale over the hole. They left no name and no wooden cross.

Three days later, the Ministry sent an augment squad down to Lower Four with steam-batons and listening horns.

Junior Surveyor Malakor walked in front, checking drift numbers off a wax slate. He was twenty-two, his eyes red from lack of sleep.

He knelt by the ditch in the nursery. The floor was dry. The infants were asleep.

Malakor pressed the brass listening horn against the floorboards. Through the tube, he heard the quiet, rhythmic purr of the auxiliary pump running smooth under three feet of black mud.

He took the horn from his ear.

Across the room, Sanna was standing by the tub with an iron spoon in her hand. She didn't look down. She looked right into his face.

The sergeant behind him cleared his throat, his steam carbine clicking on its sling. "Anything in the ditch, Surveyor?"

Malakor looked at Sanna’s red hands. He looked at the slate in his left hand.

He took his stylus and scratched through the blank space at the bottom of the wax:

`SECTOR 4 LOWER DRIFT: INSPECTED.`  
`STRUCTURE: ABANDONED MINE HEAVE.`  
`RECOVERABLE ASSETS: NONE.`

"Nothing," Malakor said, closing the slate. "Move to Tier Five."

---

Six years later, on Tier One, the Council dedicated the statue in the Great Concourse before the airlocks of *The Ark of Continuity*.

It was twenty-four feet high, cast from nine tonnes of polished bronze. It showed Brothar Varek standing straight as a pine tree, holding the Fault Maul over his shoulder like a general’s baton, his right hand holding up a bronze globe of the Mountain.

Under his boots, in letters of hammered gold, the stone read:

```
VAREK FAIRGUNJIS
HE GAVE HIS IRON THAT WE MIGHT ENDURE
```

Every morning, the schoolboys in clean gray wool stood in front of it and recited the state lines:

*"What one body yields, all Hearths preserve."*

Three miles down, in the cold damp of Lower Four where the water tables were creeping up the shale, a nine-year-old boy sat on an upturned nail keg watching an iron pipe elbow that joined the clean wash tank to the ditch drain. It had three wrench gouges across the hex and was wired to the timber with rusty copper braid.

"Mother," the boy asked. "Did the man on the statue die for the Spire?"

Sanna didn't look up from her cabbage kettle. She took the rag, wiped the black rim of the pot clean, and laid the cloth on the table.

"No," she said. "It was our iron anyway."

### 6. The Residual Machine (Epilogue)

The Ministry eventually confiscated what remained of the chassis. The miners kept what they could hide—the maul head, three hydraulic valves, the auxiliary copper pump in the nursery ditch—but the Council took the frame, the thoracic cage, and the control sub-assemblies back up the shafts.

They rebuilt an operational unit from original iron, modern replacements, and surviving control lattices.

The Council did not upload Varek. They had no technology for souls, and no interest in them. What they inherited was the mechanical residue of having known him: three centuries of an adaptive control system that had compensated for his bone fractures, anticipated his weight shifts before stone sheared, learned how he braced under a falling crown, and recorded every involuntary habit of his nervous system.

Over that residue, the Ministry laid its own layer: approved vocal synthesizers, patrol routes, work quotas, and rescue triage protocols.

So there were two systems inside the rebuilt chassis.

**Council Varek** was what they intentionally built.

**Residual Varek** was what they did not know they had kept.

The Council would log: *Proceed to Shaft Twelve.* It proceeded.  
They would command: *Rescue allocation exhausted.* It halted.

Usually.

Then once in a while, someone screamed from behind a fractured bulkhead and the machine turned its head. A technician would check the command queue. Nothing in the stack instructed it to move. It stepped toward the voice anyway.

The Hearths did not proclaim a miracle. Some believed the iron still remembered him. Some thought that was sentimental rot:

> “Varek died.”

The old timbermen would look toward the dark drift and spit:

> “Then why does it still knock?”

Neither side was ever proven right. When the Ministry engineers logged the anomalies, they had rational explanations for every twitch: bad calibration, corrupted memory blocks, missing parts kept by the lower wards, obsolete rescue heuristics overriding modern priority queues. Every strange action was defensible as mechanical decay.

That was the obscenity of it. The Council did not make the machine evil. It actually saved people. Survival rates in the lower headings improved. Newsreels showed it carrying crushed apprentices out of the dust. And every rescue reinforced the Spire’s gospel: *Varek still serves.* They took something authentically good about the man and put it back to work for the order he died rejecting.

Then decades passed.

Council modifications failed. Replacement parts stopped fitting. Approved voice libraries corrupted into static. The ceremonial gilt flaked away under sulfuric damp. The people who had seen the living man die in Lower Four grew old and were buried in the shale.

Eventually, nobody maintaining the chassis had ever seen the human being whose habits were buried in its relays.

Yet the habits remained.

It braced from the left. It reached for the second survivor first. It left its own withdrawal clearance until last. It touched door frames before entering unstable headings.

It knocked twice.

Engineers logged them as legacy routines. Miners called them Varek.

In the end, even the memory of the war faded into the dark of the mountain.

A dying, untracked machine stepped into an abandoned drift on Tier Seven. No survivors waited behind the stone. No crew worked the seam. No Ministry camera recorded the passage.

It reached a rusted blast door.

*Clang. Clang.*

Two heavy knuckle raps on the iron plate.

It waited five seconds, as if expecting an answer from an empty vault.

It levered the latch open and stepped through. It crossed the abandoned hall, found an air vent rattling against loose shale, and sealed the toggle, though there was no one left alive on the level to breathe the draft.

Then it walked to the central gallery where the roof timbers had rotted to sponge.

It stepped beneath the sagging crown. It planted its steel boots into the bedrock floor and assumed the ancient, three-tonne load-bearing stance.

The internal systems began to go dark, bank by bank. No words came from its speakers. No revelation. No memory.

Just before the last valve lost pressure, a single functioning hydraulic actuator on the left shoulder made a four-millimeter correction, shifting the entire weight of the mountain away from an empty corridor where children had played two hundred years before.

Pointless.

Unless you were Varek.

Then the iron locked cold.
