# The Weight of Shaft 44
*Acts One & Two*

---

### 1. Drift Nine

The air in Drift 9 tasted of burned copper and wet soot, dragged through miles of corroded ventilation pipe until it felt like breathing wool.

Junior Surveyor Malakor stood at Bulkhead 44 with his hands on the brass valve spindle. Under his boots, the stone floor was humming—a high, dry vibration that came up through his heels and rattled in his teeth. 

Shear.

From the brass acoustic tube mounted to the rock timbering came the sound of three miles of overburden settling: sharp cracks like dry pine boards breaking over a knee, followed by shouting from the drift below:

"—Malakor? Turn the bypass, damn you! The timber at Four is bowing. Bram’s pinned under the shaker—his leg’s under the lip—"

Malakor looked at the wall telegraph. The punch needle had just finished perforating a strip of gray card, dropping it into the iron tray below:

`DP-7B // SMELTERS COMMITTED // ACTUAL: 0.0 //`  
`BYPASS DOGS TO SECURE VENTILATION //`  
`TIE ALL LOWER FLUES // PRIORITY ONE CONTINUITY`

The choice was written on the tape. 

If he turned the spindle clockwise, the iron dogs on Bulkhead 44 closed, cutting off Shaft 44 from the intake trunk. The sulfur gas pooling in the lower workings would stay clear of the Tier Four smelters, keeping the copper furnaces burning. 

If he turned it counter-clockwise, the valve opened to the shaft, giving forty trapped hewers fresh air—and chilling the blast furnaces above. 

A furnace chill on Tier Four meant twenty thousand pounds of lost copper plate before the next shift. The Ministry didn't dock pay for men lost in a cave-in; they docked rations for missed production quotas.

"Malakor!" the brass horn screamed, spitting gray water and coal grit onto his sleeve. "The air's turning black! Turn the wheel!"

Malakor’s palms were slick with cold grease. He leaned his forehead against the damp iron of the bulkhead, twenty-two years old, listening to forty men suffocate in the dark.

Then the rock floor groaned under a heavier weight.

---

### 2. The Suit

A slow, rhythmic cadence came up the haulage drift: *clack, scrape, hiss*.

Varek came around the timber corner. The three-tonne iron rig filled the narrow drift, rust-pitted plates scraping the rock ribs on both sides. Tucked beneath the curve of the *Gren-Skildus* against the chest life-support hull, half-hidden behind the shoulder drape where he brushed it in private, old foundry letters were chiseled into the inner steel: `ORA ET LABORA`.

In his left gauntlet, the Fault Maul dragged along the track ballast, thirty kilos of hardened tool steel furrowing the gravel. His right forearm was an open steel cage housing three hydraulic talons around a heavy copper induction core.

Malakor stepped back against the timber props. He started to cite the emergency regulation for Bulkhead 44, his voice cracking like dry straw.

Varek didn't look at him. 

He reached out with his left gauntlet, closed his steel fingers over the brass spindle of the bulkhead dog, and leaned his weight into the turn.

The two-inch steel spindle sheared off inside the packing nut with a dry, sharp snap. Varek shoved the heavy iron door inward with his knee, ducked his helmet under the lintel, and stepped through into the smoke.

---

### 3. The Face

The drift was thirty fathoms down, pitched steep into the footwall. The overhead baulks—twelve-inch larch timbers pickled in creosote—were groaning under the weight of the mountain, splinters peeling off their bellies in long white ribbons.

Forty miners were packed against the solid rock at the end of the drift.

In the center of the floor, fourteen-year-old Bram was pinned beneath an eight-tonne slab of banded magnetite-ironstone. The boy was lying on his side in the gray slurry, his face gray as unbaked dough, his left shin crushed flat under the lip of the rock.

Sanna was kneeling in the mud beside him, her hands clamped over the boy’s leg, pressing a torn rag into the wound to slow the dark seep of blood into the puddle.

Three timbermen were throwing their weight against a pair of six-foot pinch bars under the slab. The steel bars bent into bows; the stone didn't move an inch. It settled another fraction with a wet squelch.

Bram gave a thin, ragged shriek that broke into a cough.

"Pull the bars," Sanna told the men, her voice flat with fury. "You're grinding the bone against the shale."

"We have to lift it!" a timberman shouted back, wiping coal mud from his forehead. "When that header cracks, the whole drift comes down!"

*THUMP.*

The drift went quiet except for the boy's breathing.

Varek came down the crawlway on one knee, dragging his iron limbs through the slush. The miners pressed themselves flat against the walls, staring at the scarred plates of the rig with the dull terror they usually kept for boiler explosions.

Varek looked down at the boy, then at Sanna’s hands in the mud. He knew the boy’s face. It was the sharp chin and wide gray eyes of the child who had stood clinging to Wulfila's coat forty days ago on the gallows platform at Azg-Haíms, before Wulfila took the leap into the dark. Wulfila had died to buy an exemption for this hearth; the state had drafted the boy anyway.

"Get back," his throat-plate buzzed.

Sanna didn't let go of the leg. "If you hit that rock with the hammer, the shock will break his pelvis."

"I'm not hitting it," Varek said.

He swung the maul with his left arm, driving the pick four inches into the basalt floor to anchor the tool like a mooring bit. He reached back with his left hand and yanked the mechanical trip on his diesel pack.

---

### 4. The Lift

The small two-stroke engine on his shoulder casing fired with a sharp, hammering exhaust, throwing blue diesel smoke against the ceiling timbers.

Varek swung his right arm forward. The three steel talons of the *Faírg-Tygil* opened, locking around the edges of the eight-tonne ironstone block. The copper induction ring inside the cuff hummed, drawing current straight from the spine generator until the brushes whined.

Pulsed magnetic flux seized the dense magnetite bands within the stone. 

The machine did not eliminate weight. Every pound of the falling slab transferred straight through the hydraulic ram on Varek’s arm into his chassis, driving his boots two inches into the fractured rock underfoot.

The pins in his right hip screamed with metal-on-metal friction. A high-pressure copper line on his forearm ruptured, spraying hot mineral oil over his quartz visor. 

Under the iron breastplate, Varek's breath came in ragged wheezes. The downward thrust drove the steel harness into his collarbone, cracking the bone beneath the leather straps.

"Pull him out," Varek rasped through his throat-plate.

Sanna got her arms under Bram’s shoulders, dug her heels into the shale, and pulled. 

The boy came free with a wet sucking sound, leaving half his trouser leg behind in the muck. Two timbermen caught him by the belt and dragged him backward through the crawlway.

Varek held the stone for four seconds while the miners scrambled past his iron knees into the clear drift. 

His right elbow sleeve warped three degrees out of true. The copper coils in the emitter began to smoke, the shellac insulation charring black under the electrical load. With a last push of his hydraulic rams, Varek shoved the ironstone slab back against the footwall, cut the diesel throttle, and dropped to both knees as the main ceiling timbers gave way behind him.

---

### 5. The Climb

The miners were cheering in the main drift, but Varek stayed on his knees in the dust. 

His right arm was dead. The copper coils had fused into a lump of blistered slag, coolant leaking through the cracked casing into his boot.

In the mud by the shattered bulkhead, he saw the strip of gray telegraph tape Malakor had dropped when he ran.

Varek picked up the paper with his left iron fingers. He held it close to his quartz viewport, reading the punched dots by the light of a flickering grease wick:

`ACOUSTIC TRACE CONFIRMED: D-19`  
`EXPECTED SHEAR: SHAFT 44`  
`COPPER COMMITTED: 820 TONS`  
`MAINTAIN DRAW TO LIMIT`

They had known the shaft was failing nineteen days ago. They had kept forty men digging in the dark to get eight hundred tons of copper out before the ceiling came down.

Varek let the paper fall into the slurry.

He got his left boot under him, straightened his knee, and turned away from the miners who were binding Bram’s leg.

He didn't speak to them. He dragged his dead right arm along the rock rib and began the long climb up the spiral haulage incline toward Tier One.
