# Notes on Thulan Political Economy
*Background Briefing: Labor, Scarcity, and the State*

---

### The Working Contradiction

The economy of Mount Faírguni is neither a pure command system nor an open market. It has spent three centuries compounding the worst habits of both. 

The state owns the stone, the air, and the extraction quotas. Private merchant houses lease the tools, manage the transport hoists, and run the distribution wickets. In practice, this means everything belongs to the collective in theory, everything costs money in practice, and an ordinary miner owns nothing at all.

Power remains concentrated because everyone inside the rock is terrified of physical scarcity: air failure, water contamination, or a collapse of the haulage lines. The bureaucracy uses this fear to demand unconditional labor quotas. The merchant guilds use it to tack on private service fees at every stage of production.

---

### Mechanics of the Grind

#### 1. The Distribution Surcharge
The Ministry of Continuity calculates regional grain allocations from Tier One. On paper, every worker has an equal life-entitlement coupon: twenty pounds of coarse turnip-and-barley meal per month. 

The state does not run the transport skips. The skips belong to licensed merchant houses like House Halza. When a family takes their ration voucher to the wicket on Tier Seven, the merchant clerk deducts:
* A transit surcharge per fathom lowered down the incline.
* A ventilation fee for keeping the cargo dry in the shafts.
* A weigh-scale handling tare.

A worker entitled to twenty pounds of flour walks away with eight. The merchant hasn't broken state law; he has simply billed for the fuel burned by the hoist engine.

#### 2. Tool Debt and the Public Pick
The Council maintains that the copper in the mountain belongs to the people, making private ownership of mineral veins illegal. Mining is framed as a sacred civic obligation.

However, the equipment needed to reach the copper is privately leased. A hewer pays out of his daily wage for:
* Carbide fuel and lamp grease.
* Pneumatic drill-bit sharpening.
* Respirator filter repacking.

A miner who works a sixteen-hour shift cutting basalt mud is officially praised as a hero of the Hearth, but leaves the shift window owing two copper marks to the contractor because his drill bit chipped against an iron nodule.

#### 3. State Targets vs. Futures Speculation
The Council sets fixed five-year copper targets to plate the hulls of the evacuation ships under construction on Tier Four. If a drift misses its target, the district’s meat rations are canceled.

On Tiers Two and Three, merchant cartels trade the extraction scrip on paper. When an accident or fault shear occurs—such as the collapse of Shaft 44—the sudden supply choke causes the spot price of existing copper warrants to skyrocket. Hewers are crushed in the dark trying to meet an unbending state deadline, while speculators three tiers above them make fortunes off the shortage caused by the disaster.

### Tier Strata

* **Tiers 1–2 (The Upper Spire):** Administrative offices, registry archives, merchant banking houses. Filtered air, electric lamps, subsidized bread made from clean grain. The people living here manage files and ledger entries; they rarely see unworked rock or breathe sulfur damp.
* **Tiers 3–4 (Foundries and Heavy Works):** Blast furnaces, rolling mills, machine shops. Extreme heat, deafening noise, iron dust. The work is dangerous, but the mechanics and smiths have trade guilds that protect them from the worst abuses.
* **Tiers 5–6 (Timber Flues and Mid-Levels):** Timber-felling gangs working fossilized trunks and bracket-fungus farms; haulage skip operators. Sawdust and rope grease. High injury rates from snapping hoist cables.
* **Tiers 7–8 (The Sinks and Lower Hearths):** Unskilled labor, sump drainage, exploratory drilling, slag sorting. Constant standing water, black-damp, sulfur gas. Child shaker-hands, widespread lung-rot, and near-total indebtedness to local tool-shops.

### The Culture of the Hearth

Food is where the class divide is felt most bitterly.

In the Spire, waste is casual. Bread crusts are trimmed off and burned as fuel because stale bread is unpalatable to senior clerks. 

In the lower drifts, caloric poverty has produced an obsessive culinary discipline. Nothing that can be rendered is thrown away. Bones are simmered for two days to leach out marrow grease; wild leek roots are charred to mask the taste of swamp mud; ditch eels are preserved in sour vinegar crocks. A meal plated on a roofing slate by a drift woman using spoiled rations often requires ten times more labor and technical skill than the boiled mutton served at a Council dinner. 

Wiping the rim of a tin plate with a clean cloth before eating is not an attempt to mimic the rich; it is a miner's refusal to live like cattle in the mud.

---

### Why the Structure Holds

The system does not survive merely by force of arms; it survives because both halves have genuine historic utility that people remember:

1. **The State Scale:** Only central planning could have surveyed, blasted, and lined the Great North Aqueduct or the thirty-mile intake flues that keep forty million people from suffocating in stagnant air.
2. **The Private Fix:** When state-issued bilge pumps failed because an engineer in the Spire specified the wrong bronze alloy, local guild smiths turned out custom hardened-steel impellers on their own lathes to keep the lower drifts from drowning.

Because both institutions have saved the population from disaster in living memory, people tolerate the daily cruelty of both. The bureaucrat claims the quota is the only thing keeping the air scrubbers running. The merchant claims his fees are the only thing keeping the hoists lubricated.

---

### Varek's Position

Varek Fairgunjis does not fit into this balance. He was bolted into his suit three centuries ago, before the guilds took over the tools and before the Ministry turned survival into a set of casualty percentages.

He does not respect the state's quotas. When he tears an auxiliary pump from his own frame to drop into a flooded ditch, he is destroying military property that belongs to the evacuation fleet.

He does not respect private debt. When he cracks Bulkhead 44 with his maul to vent an unapproved shaft, he ruins a piece of infrastructure leased by a merchant house.

He represents an older, simpler rule that threatens both sides: that a machine belongs to the hands that use it, and that no man should be asked to die for an equation he isn't allowed to read.

This is why, after his death, the Ministry cannot admit what happened. If the lower drifts learned that an ancient Thulan tore himself apart to give his iron to miners with pipe wrenches, the authority of the quotas would break. By casting him in bronze and turning him into a symbol of obedient self-sacrifice, the Council takes the man who defied them and uses his memory to enforce the very system he rejected.
